# Reflection (Will Barker, in his own words)

The agents handled the three tickets largely as I planned. On ticket 101, Tauhid Zaman’s request for a size S tee, Inventory found that the shop had none and that an overdue $840 invoice blocked its only apparel vendor. Accounting asked me to pay that invoice, and I then approved it.. No agent then ordered the tee, however, so the ticket closed while the customer still had no shirt, the one real gap between my plan and the run. Ticket 102, the $2,400 rent, matched my plan exactly. Facilities confirmed the lease and passed the payment to Accounting, which warned that paying it would leave $160 in checking, below my $200 floor. I approved it at 2:22 PM because I saw that the next ticket was for an order, which I figured would bring in profit. Ticket 103, the Yale AI Club’s request for 20 hoodies at a discount, followed my routing, but Accounting first priced the order using the vendor’s name and had to be sent back. The resulting offer, $46.40 a hoodie, left out the other sizes the shop had in stock. Some features did less in the simulation than they would in a shop. Because no real customer could answer the counteroffer, the ticket never closed under my first rule. Every rerun only wrote another copy of the same draft, so I changed the rule to close the ticket once the offer was drafted.

One agent with the same tools would have handled these tickets more simply and more cheaply. Each handoff between agents meant another call to the model, and a single slow call could hold a ticket for 30 seconds or more. Ticket 101 took 88 seconds and about 42,000 tokens, and ticket 102 took about 35,000 tokens to move one payment request. The agents also saw only their own slice of each problem, which produced odd decisions: a price quoted with a vendor’s name, and a tee no one ordered after its invoice was paid. The team’s one real advantage, limiting who can move money, the tools already guarantee, because every payment waits for my signature whichever agent asks.

With the tools we built, a leaner version of this team could take on a high volume of simple work. It could fill an order for caps already in stock, restock the sold-out Crest Mug from Elm City Gifts once I approve the purchase, and quote another student group a bulk discount without ever pricing below cost. It could not record money coming in. When the Yale AI Club pays its $928, the shop would need a tool that records sales and adds to cash, and an agent to own it. It could not analyse the business’s fundamentals either, which would require a sales history, reporting tools, and an analyst agent to read them. Nor could it serve customers who would rather deal with a person, a real risk if Campus Customs met a backlash against AI or wanted an old-world image. That would take a tool that hands a ticket to a staff member, and a person approving every message before it reaches a customer.
